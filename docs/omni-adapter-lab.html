    Omni Adapter Lab

    A working guide to bolting vision and audio onto a small text LLM, then giving it a voice. It covers what an adapter is, which designs exist and what each one trades off, how to wire one into Qwen3, how to train it in stages, how to add another modality without breaking the first, and how a Talker turns the model's thoughts into speech. The panels are live: change a setting in one place and token counts, parameter counts, memory and cost update everywhere.

      Foundations

      The mental model

      Qwen3 only understands one thing: a sequence of 1024-dimensional vectors, normally looked up from its embedding table. To make it "see" or "hear", you produce more vectors that live in the same space and slot them into the sequence. The LLM then processes them like any other tokens.

      The encoder (SigLIP2 for images, Whisper for audio) already knows how to turn pixels or sound into rich features, but in its own space and at its own resolution. The adapter is the translator between the two. It has exactly two jobs:

      1. Change the dimension. Encoder width (768, 1152, 1280…) becomes Qwen3's 1024, and the vectors land where the LLM expects meaning to be.
      2. Change the length. 1024 image patches or 1500 audio frames become a token count the LLM can afford. Every extra token costs attention compute, KV-cache memory and context window.

      Every adapter design in this guide is a different answer to the second job: how to throw away length while keeping the information the LLM needs.

      Why this works at allPre-trained encoders and LLMs learn surprisingly compatible representations. A small adapter trained on a few hundred thousand caption pairs is enough to make an LLM describe images. That is why the LLaVA recipe is the standard starting point, and why the adapter is the part worth studying.

      The senders
      Encoders: where features come from
      
      
      
        
      Encoders stay frozen in almost every stage. You read their hidden states, and the main choices are which checkpoint and which layer.
      
        
        
          
      • Use the penultimate layer for vision. SigLIP's last layer is shaped by its contrastive training objective. hidden_states[-2] usually keeps more spatial detail, which is why LLaVA uses it. Compare it with -1 and a middle layer as an experiment.
        • 
          
      • Whisper always pads to 30 s. Its encoder emits 1500 frames (50 per second) no matter how short the clip is. Slice off the padding frames before the adapter, or a 3-second question costs 1500 tokens.
        • 
          
      • Mel bins differ. Whisper small and medium use 80 mel bins; large-v3 and turbo use 128. Always use the matching feature extractor.

      Compression, visualised
      Pixel shuffle and frame stacking
      
      
      Both tricks compress length without discarding anything. Neighbouring vectors are concatenated into one wider vector, so the token count drops by the factor and the width grows by the same factor. The MLP that follows decides what to keep. This is why they beat pooling on detail-heavy tasks such as OCR or fast speech.

      Sequence composition
      
          
      KV cache = tokens × 28 layers × 2 (K,V) × 8 heads × 128 × 2 bytes. Compute ≈ 2 × 0.44B × tokens for the dense part; the attention part grows with tokens², shown as a ratio to text-only. Adapter parameter counts include biases and norms.
      
        
      
      
      
      
      What to noticePixel shuffle makes the token count small but the adapter's first layer huge (input width × r²). That is the real trade-off: compute moves out of the LLM's attention and into a cheap per-token MLP. Perceiver adapters stay small and give a fixed M, but they mix all patches together, so fine spatial detail is the first thing lost.

      The silent killer
      Scale matching
      
      
      
        
      Qwen3 is a pre-norm transformer. Each layer reads an RMS-normalised copy of the residual stream and adds its output back. That has a subtle consequence for multimodal tokens. A token's own attention inputs are normalised, so its raw magnitude does not change its own query and key directly. What the magnitude does control is how much the following layers can change that token.
      
        
        
          
      • Too loud (adapter output norm ≫ text embedding norm): layer updates are small next to the raw vector, so the token stays close to its input for many layers. The LLM struggles to "process" it, and training is often unstable at first.
        • 
          
      • Too quiet (≪ text norm): the first layer's update swamps it, so the content gets overwritten before deeper layers can use it.
        • 
        
      
        
      The fix is cheap: measure both norms, then end the adapter with a norm layer (Ultravox ends in RMSNorm) or a learned scale. Log the ratio during training.

      Extending
      Adding another adapter without breaking the first
      
      
      
        
      Say images already work and you add audio. Isolation is the rule: the new modality gets its own encoder, adapter and placeholder token, so stage 1 cannot affect the old path. Nothing it depends on has trainable weights. The risk only appears in stage 2, when shared LLM weights change. You have two strategies there:
      
      
      
      
      
        
      A · Shared fine-tuning on mixed data
      Unfreeze the LLM and train on a mix of the new modality, the old modality and text-only data. It is simple and lets modalities help each other, but you must re-check the old modality's evals after every run.
      
        
      B · One LoRA per modality (Phi-4-multimodal style)
      The LLM stays frozen and each modality gets its own LoRA. Old capabilities are untouched by construction. The open question is samples with both image and audio: activate both LoRAs, or train a third "mixed" one. That makes a good experiment.

        Voice out
        Talking: from Thinker to Talker
        
      
        
        
        Everything so far gives the model eyes and ears, but it still answers in text. Talking adds a mouth. A model can't predict raw sound directly, because a waveform is 16,000 to 24,000 numbers per second. So speech output uses the same trick as text: turn sound into a short stream of discrete sound-letters and predict those.
        
        
          
          
        1. Codec (frozen, just added). A pre-trained neural codec squashes speech into codec tokens, roughly 12 to 50 per second, and can turn them back into a waveform. You plug it in the same way as Whisper.
          2. 
          
        2. Talker (new, trained from scratch). A small transformer reads the Thinker's hidden states and writes codec tokens, the way an LLM writes words.
          3. 
          
        3. Codec decoder. Turns the Talker's tokens into sound you can hear.
          4. 
        
        
        
        The Thinker (Qwen3 plus adapters) stays frozen while the Talker trains, so learning to speak can't damage understanding. Qwen2.5-Omni separates the two in the same way and calls them Thinker and Talker.
        
      
        

      
        
        
        Sound-letter explorer · one second of speech
          
        
        
        
          
        
          
        
            
        
          
        
          
        
        
        
      
        
      
        Start with one codebookWith a single-codebook codec (XCodec2, or CosyVoice2-style semantic tokens), the Talker writes one stream of tokens, exactly like a text LLM, so your Qwen-side knowledge carries straight over. Multi-codebook codecs (Mimi, SNAC) need several tokens per frame, which means a delay pattern, parallel heads or a small "depth" transformer. Leave those for your second Talker.

        OptionHow it worksWhat you trainTrade-off
        
        
          
        A · TTS cascadeThinker writes text; an off-the-shelf TTS reads it aloud.nothingWorks on day one and sounds good. The voice can't react to tone, and it waits for text before speaking.
        
          
        B · CTC speech head
        LLaMA-Omni style        		A small head upsamples the Thinker's hidden states and predicts discrete speech units for all positions at once (CTC); a unit vocoder makes sound.~tens of MCheapest real mouth, and fast because nothing is generated step by step.         C · Autoregressive Talker
        Qwen-Omni / Mini-Omni style        		A small transformer writes codec tokens one by one, conditioned on the Thinker's states, streaming while the Thinker is still writing.~100–200MThe full design: natural prosody and low latency.         How the Talker reads the Thinker
        
      
        
        
        The Talker never sees the image or the audio input. It sees the Thinker's hidden states for the answer tokens: one 1024-d vector per text token, carrying what to say and some of how to say it. These are projected to the Talker's width and placed before the audio tokens as a prefix. The Talker then predicts codec tokens with ordinary next-token cross-entropy.
        
        
          
          
        • Prefix version (start here): the Talker reads the whole answer, then speaks. It is simple and trains easily, but it can't start until the text is finished.
          • 
          
        • Streaming version: interleave chunks, for example a few text states, then the audio tokens for them, then the next text chunk. The Talker stays a few tokens behind the Thinker and starts speaking almost at once. That gap is the "text lookahead" in the latency panel.
          • 
          
        • Which Thinker layer: the last layer is shaped for predicting the next word, while a middle layer may carry more about tone. Try both as an experiment.

        Making the training data
        
      
        
        
          
          
        1. Collect text answers: the assistant turns from your stage-2 data, or your Thinker's own answers.
          2. 
          
        2. Speak each answer with a good open TTS model on Modal, in one fixed voice. A single consistent speaker is far easier for a small Talker to learn. Check the TTS model's licence for this use.
          3. 
          
        3. Encode the audio with the codec into token ids and save them to the Volume. This is the Talker's target.
          4. 
          
        4. Run the frozen Thinker over prompt plus answer and keep the hidden states at the answer positions. This is the Talker's input. Cache it, or compute it on the fly.
          5. 
          
        5. Train the Talker. Every few hundred steps, decode a few samples and listen to them.
          6. 
          
        6. Evaluate with round-trip WER (Whisper transcribes the Talker's speech and you compare it with the text answer), a naturalness score such as UTMOS, and time to first sound.
          7.
